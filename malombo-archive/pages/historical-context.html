Competing meanings of “Malombo”

Within Vhavenda ritual life, Malombo names a healing system structured through possession, drumming, chant, dance, and embodied transformation. In apartheid-era urban performance, the same term also came to name Philip Tabane’s musical language, producing a tension between lived ritual meaning and public classification [3].

1960s South Africa

Violent urban transformation

Forced removals, labor migration, racial segregation, and township circulation fractured older communal structures while generating new forms of urban musical exchange.

Colonial discourse

Retribalisation and reduction

As Griselda Felber argues, colonial ethnography often froze African practice into simplified ethnic categories, making dynamic ritual systems appear static or premodern.

Ethnomusicology and lived practice

Documentation mattered, but it never exhausted the practice itself. Blacking’s work is important precisely because it treated Venda music as embedded social action rather than isolated sound, even while the broader colonial archive continued to sort African expressive life into externally imposed categories [4].

Urban circulation rather than abandonment

Malombo moved through shebeens, township stages, radio, and recording studios without becoming merely symbolic. The venue changed; the deeper logic of invocation, repetition, and ancestral continuity did not.

John Blacking

Blacking’s fieldwork remains pivotal because it positioned Venda music inside social relation, ritual effectiveness, and embodied meaning. That move challenged earlier tendencies to treat possession as primitivism, superstition, or abnormal psychology [5].

Philip Tabane

Tabane’s intervention was historical as well as musical. Raised in a family connected to healing traditions, he described performance as communication with ancestral forces rather than entertainment alone. Critics called this music jazz because that was the nearest available urban category, but Tabane’s resistance to the label preserved its ritual genealogy inside modern circulation [6].
