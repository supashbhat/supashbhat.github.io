Theoretical Framework

This project reads Malombo not as symbolic folklore or aesthetic residue, but as a performative system in which sound acts upon reality, reorganizes bodily attention, and mediates ancestral presence.

Music as action, not representation

The final paper argues that Malombo music should be understood as a system of action. Rhythm, chant, repetition, and movement do not merely symbolize an invisible reality; they help produce the conditions through which ancestral presence becomes perceptible and effective. In this sense, sound becomes a social force rather than a decorative accompaniment to ritual [1].

This framework matters because colonial accounts often treated African possession as superstition, pathology, or spectacle. By contrast, Blacking, Rouget, Turner, and Davhula make it possible to read Malombo as a culturally organized process in which repetition, embodiment, and communal interpretation generate transformation rather than irrational excess [2].

Ethnomusicology

John Blacking

Shows that Venda music cannot be separated from social meaning, bodily participation, and ritual effectiveness. His work helps reject the treatment of possession as pathology.

Trance Theory

Gilbert Rouget

Rejects the simplistic claim that rhythm mechanically causes trance. Possession becomes intelligible only within a system of expectation, embodiment, and interpretation.

Ritual Process

Victor Turner

Clarifies how Malombo creates liminal space, suspending ordinary roles and producing communitas through collective rhythmic intensity.

Indigenous Healing

Mudzunga Junniah Davhula

Places drumming, cyclical vocality, movement, and repetition at the center of healing, naming Malombo as a sonic bridge between visible and invisible worlds.
